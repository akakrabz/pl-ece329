{{#params.facts}} {{{text}}} {{/params.facts}}
The law is always true; it gives $\mathbf{E}$ only when symmetry lets you pick a closed surface made of pieces on which $\mathbf{E}$ is either normal with constant magnitude or tangential: a concentric sphere, a coaxial cylinder (end caps carry no flux) or a pillbox (sides carry no flux; both caps count). On a concentric sphere of radius $r$, $\epsilon_0 E_r\,4\pi r^2 = Q_{\text{enc}}(r)$. Inside a uniform ball only the fraction $(r/a)^3$ of its charge is enclosed; a uniform shell contributes only to spheres that enclose it.

(b) The answer is {{{params.gauss_answer}}}. {{{params.gauss_why}}} Each of the other listed distributions is spherically, cylindrically or planarly symmetric (a density that varies only with the distance from the centre, axis or mid-plane keeps the symmetry), so a sphere, coaxial cylinder or pillbox turns $\oint\mathbf{D}\cdot d\mathbf{S}$ into $D\times$area. "Gauss's law holds for every surface" is true, but holding is not the same as being solvable for $\mathbf{E}$.

(c) By symmetry $\mathbf{E} = E_r(r)\,\hat{r}$, so a concentric Gaussian sphere of radius $r$ gives $E_r = \dfrac{Q_{\text{enc}}(r)}{4\pi\epsilon_0 r^2}$, with $\dfrac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0} = 8.988\times10^{9}\ \mathrm{m/F}$:

  • $r_1 = {{params.r1}}$ cm (inside the ball): $Q_{\text{enc}} = Q_b\,(r_1/a)^3 = ({{params.Qb}})({{params.frac1_str}}) = {{params.Qenc1_str}}\ \mathrm{nC}$, so $E_r = {{params.E1_str}}\ \mathrm{kV/m}$. (Equivalently $E_r = Q_b r_1/(4\pi\epsilon_0 a^3)$.)
  • $r_2 = {{params.r2}}$ cm (between the ball and the shell): $Q_{\text{enc}} = Q_b = {{params.Qb}}\ \mathrm{nC}$, so $E_r = {{params.E2_str}}\ \mathrm{kV/m}$.
  • $r_3 = {{params.r3}}$ cm (outside the shell): $Q_{\text{enc}} = Q_b + Q_s = {{params.Qout_str}}\ \mathrm{nC}$, so $E_r = {{params.E3_str}}\ \mathrm{kV/m}$.
